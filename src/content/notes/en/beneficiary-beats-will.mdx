Proceeds go to the beneficiary named on the policy — not to whoever your will names. Whatever the will says, whatever the divorce decree says, the insurer follows that line on the contract. Designations left unchanged after a marriage, divorce, or birth are the single most common problem I see.

## Fill in the contingent, not just the primary

If the primary dies first, or in the same accident, and no contingent is named, the money falls into the estate. That means probate, months of delay, and exposure to creditors — losing the one thing a death benefit is supposed to do well, which is pay quickly.

## Don't name a minor child directly

A minor cannot receive proceeds. The money sits until a court appoints a guardian, and then it is handed over in full on their eighteenth birthday. Naming a trust, or appointing a custodian, is the standard fix.

<Callout tag="Five-minute check">

Log into the insurer's portal, open the beneficiary screen, and verify spelling, dates of birth, and percentages — they must total 100%. Group coverage through your employer carries its own separate designation; check that one too.

</Callout>

## Revisit it whenever this happens

- Marriage, divorce, remarriage
- A birth or an adoption
- The death of a named beneficiary
- A job change — group coverage does not follow you

## Added later — two questions families ask afterward

*Added in November 2026.*

### Is the payout taxed?

Usually not. The IRS says life insurance proceeds you receive as a beneficiary because the insured person died generally **aren't included in your income, and you don't have to report them**. If the money is paid out over time and earns **interest**, though, that interest is taxable and should be reported as interest income. If the amount is large, a quick check with a tax professional is worth the peace of mind.

### We don't know what policies they had

This happens more than people think — especially with old policies or papers that were never organized. The **NAIC** (the association of state insurance regulators) runs a **Life Insurance Policy Locator**, and it is **free**.

- You enter the deceased's Social Security number (or ITIN), legal name, date of birth, date of death, and your relationship to them. Keep the death certificate next to you
- Insurers check that information against their own records. If a policy exists **and you are the beneficiary, the company contacts you directly**
- If nothing is found, or you are not the beneficiary, you won't hear back

<Callout tag="What you can do now">

Spare your family that search: write down each policy's company, policy number and your agent's contact on one sheet, and keep it with your will. Do it alongside the beneficiary check in this article — five minutes covers both.

</Callout>
