> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, September 1, 2026.

Over two weeks, you have pinned down your gap. Now it is time to look at what you have to close it with.

In my consultations, surprisingly few people can say right away what all their assets add up to. Not because they don't have any — because they're scattered. Change jobs a few times, open accounts here and there, buy a house, run a business, and that's how it naturally ends up.

Today, let's bring what's scattered onto one page. Do just this, and you've done half of your retirement preparation.

## 1. Five lines are enough

Take out a sheet of paper and write these five lines. First, the 401k at your current job. Second, any 401k left behind at a previous job. Third, IRAs. Fourth, bank deposits and CDs. Fifth, your home and your business.

Next to each line, just write a rough amount. It doesn't need to be exact. The point isn't accounting; it's seeing the whole picture at a glance — for the first time in your life.

If you already have life insurance or an annuity, add one more line for it.

I recommend doing this with your spouse, sitting down together. Often each of you knows about different accounts. If something suddenly happens to one of you and the other doesn't know what is where, that becomes another serious problem in itself.

## 2. You may have an account you've forgotten

Look especially at the second line: the 401k from a previous job. It is extremely common to leave one behind when changing jobs and not touch it for years. That money sits there without you knowing what it's invested in or how much is going out in fees every year.

If your old employer was acquired or went out of business, you may not even know where the account went. Those accounts can be found too. Even if it doesn't look like much, it's well worth checking. I've seen accounts left alone for over a decade grow into more than people expected, many times.

Checking isn't hard. Contact your former employer's HR department or the financial company that managed the 401k at the time. If the company no longer exists, you can use the federal government's lookup service for unclaimed retirement savings.

## 3. Draw one more line at the bottom

When you're done, draw one more line at the bottom and write: "Of all this, how much turns into income that arrives every single month?"

Most people's pens stop here. They have savings, but apart from Social Security, nothing that comes in monthly.

This is the heart of retirement preparation. You don't retire on the size of your assets; you retire on the income that arrives each month. Even with a million dollars, if you don't know how much it produces each month, the anxiety doesn't go away. In fact, I often see people with substantial assets who fear retirement more — because the number is big, but they don't know how much of it comes back to them each month.

## [Plus tip] Assets and income are not the same thing

Owning a house doesn't mean the house pays your monthly living costs. Even a large balance in a 401k is just a number until you decide when to start drawing, how much, and in what order.

On the other hand, money set up to pay a fixed amount every month for life plays a big role even if it's small, because that amount arrives whether the market goes up or down. Why turning assets into income sits at the center of retirement planning is something I'll cover in earnest in the middle of this series.

## Closing

This week's homework is one sheet of paper. Write the five lines, and draw one more line at the bottom. That last line sets the direction from here.

Starting next week, we'll cover the first pillar of that income: Social Security. When you claim it makes a big difference in what you receive over your lifetime. We'll work out together which is better — 62, 67 or 70.
