> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, September 15, 2026.

In my consultations, couples often sit side by side and tell me, "My Social Security is $2,000 a month and my wife's is $900, so together that's $2,900 — we'll get by." The addition is right. But that $2,900 only comes in while both of you are alive.

Social Security has two features that apply only to married couples. One is the spousal benefit; the other is the survivor benefit. If you don't know about them and each of you calculates only your own record, half of your retirement plan is left empty.

These features matter most when there's a big gap between spouses in earnings or in age. Today, let's take last week's question — when to claim — and look at it again through the eyes of a couple.

## 1. Even with a thin record of your own, you can receive up to half of your spouse's

Many people have a small Social Security record of their own because they kept the household all their lives or worked part-time. That's where the spousal benefit comes in. It can pay up to 50% of your spouse's benefit at full retirement age; if the amount calculated on your own record is lower, it makes up the difference.

For example, if a husband's full-retirement-age benefit is $2,000 a month, his wife can receive up to $1,000 even if her own record pays $900. But that 50% assumes she claims at her own full retirement age. Claim early at 62 and it drops to around 32.5% — and once reduced, it stays at that amount for life.

One more point. Your own retirement benefit grows each year you wait, up to 70, but the spousal benefit does not grow if you wait past full retirement age. If the spousal benefit is what you're after, you only need to wait until full retirement age.

## 2. When one of you passes first, two checks become one

This is the part of a couple's plan that gets missed most often. When one spouse passes away, the survivor receives not both benefits but only the larger of the two. The smaller check disappears.

Take the household from the example above, where $3,000 came in each month: $2,000 for the husband and $1,000 for the wife. If the husband passes first, what remains for the wife is the single $2,000 he was receiving. Living costs barely drop, but a third of the income is gone.

The survivor benefit the remaining spouse receives can be up to 100% — the full amount the deceased spouse was actually receiving. However, if the survivor claims early at 60, it drops to around 71.5%; claiming at the full retirement age for survivor benefits (67 for those born in 1962 or later) pays 100%.

## 3. The higher earner's claiming age decides both spouses' retirement

This is where last week's discussion and the couple's view meet. If the higher-earning spouse waits until 70, the larger benefit isn't used only during that spouse's lifetime. It becomes the basis for the surviving spouse's benefit.

Conversely, if the higher earner hurries to claim at 62 and locks in a smaller amount, the survivor benefit is smaller by the same amount, for life. All the more if one of you is in poor health: the higher earner claiming later is what protects the one left behind.

A common combination is for the lower earner to claim early and create cash flow for the couple, while the higher earner holds out as long as possible. The right answer differs for every couple, but you only see it when both of you are on the same sheet.

## [Plus tip] If you're still working, your benefit may be reduced

If you receive Social Security before full retirement age while working, then as of 2026, benefits are reduced by $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480 a year. In the year you reach full retirement age, the threshold rises to $65,160 and the reduction eases to $1 for every $3.

The reduced amount isn't lost forever; it's taken into account after full retirement age. But your cash flow for the next few years can turn out different from what you expected. If you have self-employment income, be sure to run the numbers before claiming early.

## Closing

There's one thing to do this week. Put both spouses' Social Security statements (ssa.gov) side by side and write down each full-retirement-age amount. Then calculate whether you could live on just the larger of the two. Wherever the answer gets stuck is the space you'll need to fill in the next step.
