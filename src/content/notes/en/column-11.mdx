> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, October 27, 2026.

"If my mother goes into a nursing home, Medicare pays for all of it, doesn't it?" When someone who has just begun caring for a parent asks me this, my heart sinks, because in most cases it doesn't. Medicare is insurance for treating illness, not insurance that takes long-term responsibility for care that helps with daily life.

Today I'll pick up the story I previewed at the end of September while covering when to enroll in Medicare. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services estimates that about 7 in 10 people who reach 65 will need some form of long-term care in their lifetime. In other words, this isn't someone else's problem.

## 1. Medicare pays only for "treatment"

The cases where Medicare pays for a care facility are narrow. You must be formally admitted to a hospital and stay at least three days, then move to a skilled nursing facility because you need rehabilitation or skilled nursing care. The first 20 days are covered in full; from day 21 through day 100, you pay a daily copayment; after that, coverage ends. Even that applies only while treatment aimed at recovery continues.

Care that helps with daily life — bathing, dressing, eating, using the toilet, getting out of bed — is not covered by Medicare no matter how long it's needed. The same goes for someone with dementia who needs supervision at home. Medicare supplement insurance (Medigap) barely fills this part either. One family I met in consultation had a father who spent three months in a rehabilitation facility after hip surgery. When he was judged to be no longer improving, the full cost was billed to the family starting the following month.

## 2. The costs are bigger than you think, and they come out of your own assets

It varies widely by region and facility, but bringing in a caregiver at home for six hours a day runs around $6,000 a month, and a nursing home can exceed $100,000 a year. Just three years comes close to $300,000. Compare that with the first-year required withdrawal you worked out in Part 10, and you'll feel the weight of this number.

Medicaid (ALTCS in Arizona) does support long-term care, but only if your income and assets are below certain levels. Some people think they can simply move assets into their children's names ahead of time, but that isn't easy, because the application looks back at transfers of property over the five years before you apply. Without preparation, the system is set up so that you spend the assets you saved over a lifetime first, and only then receive help.

## 3. There are three ways to prepare

The first is to cover it with your own assets. If you have ample assets, you set aside a portion for care costs. The second is long-term care insurance. Premiums are lower and underwriting is easier to pass when you apply while young and healthy, but you should know in advance that premiums can go up later. The third is adding a care benefit to life insurance or an annuity. If you need care, you use that money; if you don't, you can leave it to your family.

Which one is right depends on the size of your assets, your health and your family situation. What matters is choosing while you're healthy. After a diagnosis, your options shrink sharply.

## [Plus tip] Start by checking the insurance you already have

Surprisingly often, a life insurance policy you already own has a rider for chronic illness or care. Take out the policy and check the "riders" section, or call the insurer and ask, "Is there a provision that lets me receive the death benefit early if I need care?" Also, if you have a Health Savings Account (HSA), you can pay part of your long-term care insurance premiums from it. The amount allowed is set by age and adjusted every year.

## Closing

This week's homework is writing two things on a sheet of paper. One is "If I need care, who will look after me, and where?" The other is "Which pocket will pay three years of that cost?" It's even better to write them with your spouse. If any space is left blank, that's what you'll need to fill over the next ten years.
