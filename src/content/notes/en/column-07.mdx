> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, September 29, 2026.

"Doesn't Medicare just start on its own when my birthday comes?" It's a question I often hear from people approaching 65. You've had Medicare tax taken out of every paycheck your whole working life, so it's natural to think so. In reality, it's only half true.

For some people, the card arrives in the mail when their birthday comes. Others have to submit the application themselves. Miss the difference and a penalty follows — and that penalty isn't paid once and done; it follows you every month for life.

Last week, we looked at whether to claim Social Security at 62, 67 or 70. If you've decided to wait until 70, pay especially close attention today, because Social Security and Medicare don't move together.

## 1. Who is enrolled automatically, and who must apply

There's one test: whether you were already receiving Social Security at least four months before turning 65. If so, Part A and Part B start automatically based on the month of your birthday without a separate application, and the card arrives in the mail three months ahead.

On the other hand, if you haven't claimed Social Security yet — especially if, as discussed last week, you've decided to wait until 67 or 70 — you are not enrolled automatically. You have to apply yourself, within a set period. The point people miss most is that delaying Social Security does not delay the Medicare deadline along with it.

## 2. Miss it, and the penalty isn't once — it's for life

The enrollment period is seven months: the three months before the month of your 65th birthday, that month, and the three months after. If you don't sign up within it and have no valid reason such as employer group coverage, a penalty is added to your Part B premium. For each full 12 months you're late, 10% of the standard premium is added, and the penalty stays for as long as you keep Part B. The standard premium for 2026 is $202.90 a month; picture the penalty stacked on top of that every month.

Part D, the prescription drug coverage, has its own penalty. If you go more than 63 days without drug coverage after becoming eligible, 1% of the national base premium is added each month for every month of the gap, and that too lasts for life. Keep in mind that neither is a one-time fine: both are built into every Medicare bill you'll receive from then on, a little at a time.

## 3. The trap of "I have coverage through work, so I'm fine"

If at 65 you are still working for a company with 20 or more employees and keeping its group coverage, you can delay Part B without a penalty. In that case, you have eight months from the day you retire or the employer coverage ends to sign up.

The problem comes after that. COBRA continuation coverage after retirement, retiree plans and Marketplace plans mostly don't qualify for this delay. In my consultations, I regularly meet people who thought, "I have insurance, so it must be fine," and later found they had missed the enrollment period. Don't guess whether your coverage is the kind that lets you delay Medicare; confirm it directly.

## [Plus tip] Mark your calendar three months before you turn 65

Count back exactly three months from the month of your birthday and start the application that day. Even if you aren't receiving Social Security, you can apply for Medicare alone online at ssa.gov. When you're done, keep the confirmation number or a screenshot, and if you're married, check your spouse's timing separately. If your birthdays differ, your enrollment periods come at different times too.

## Closing

Medicare isn't something that follows along automatically like Social Security; it's a process you have to take care of yourself within a set period. However many years you have until 65, simply writing on your calendar today when your enrollment period begins is enough for this week.
