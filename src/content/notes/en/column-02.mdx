> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, August 25, 2026.

If you wrote down the three lines last week, the gap probably came out bigger than you expected. Most people then do the same thing: they quietly lower their living costs, telling themselves, "I won't spend like this once I retire."

It feels much better. But from that moment, the whole plan starts to drift. If you ask me what the most common mistake in retirement planning is, I'd say it isn't failing to prepare — it's underestimating spending. People who haven't prepared at least know they aren't ready. People who lowballed their spending believe they are.

Here are three things I've seen in practice about spending after retirement.

## 1. New expenses outweigh the ones that disappear

Some costs clearly go away when you retire: gas for the commute, work clothes, the lunch you bought every day, and the monthly retirement contribution stops. Look only at those, and yes, living costs go down.

But new ones appear. More free time means more chances to spend. The trips you postponed, money for the grandchildren, long-delayed home repairs, a new hobby. It isn't rare to see spending in the first few years of retirement rise above what it was while working.

That's because the years right after retirement are when you're still healthy and have the most you want to do. After thirty years of work, no one can tell you not to enjoy it. It just needs to be in the plan.

On top of that, large expenses that are hard to plan for — a child's wedding, caring for a parent — can land at the same time. In Korean families, this happens especially often.

## 2. The biggest variable is health insurance

Medicare starts at 65. The problem is before that. Retire at 62 or 63 and there's a two- to three-year gap until Medicare, during which you pay the full health insurance premium yourself — the same premium your employer used to cover more than half of.

Every year I see people who left those few years out of their math and end up pushing retirement back. If you're thinking of retiring early, calculate this item separately, without fail.

It doesn't end at 65, either. Medicare has its own monthly premiums and out-of-pocket costs, and there are areas basic Medicare doesn't cover, like dental and vision. I'll give that its own installment later in the series.

## 3. Prices rise quietly, but surely

This is the most underestimated item in retirement planning. To live in twenty years the way $2,000 lets you live today, you'll need a lot more money.

Social Security at least comes with a cost-of-living adjustment. The money you draw from your own savings has no such mechanism. Retirement isn't a day or two; it's a 20- or 30-year plan, and over that long stretch, prices never take a break.

If prices rise just 3% a year, in twenty years something that costs $100 today will cost more than $180. That's why a plan made in the first year of retirement can't still work unchanged twenty years later.

## [Plus tip] Which mistake would you rather make?

Lowballing your spending brings relief now. But you pay for that relief around year ten of retirement, when going back to work is far harder than it is today.

Now suppose you prepared generously and ended up spending less. That money is still there. You can travel more, or pass it on to your children and grandchildren. Which mistake is better is clear.

## Closing

Living costs after retirement don't shrink; the items change. Something drops out on one side and something fills in on the other — and the side that fills in is usually bigger.

Take out the gap you wrote down last week. If you felt the urge to lower it, raise it a little instead. Next week, we'll gather the materials for closing that gap — everything you have now — onto one page.
