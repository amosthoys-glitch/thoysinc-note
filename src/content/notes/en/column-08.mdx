> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, October 6, 2026.

Not long ago, someone who came to my office said, "My retirement is all set, but one thing scares me. What if the stock market collapses right after I retire?" Looking at a 401(k) balance can feel reassuring, yet on days when the news shows the market lurching, that reassurance turns into anxiety in an instant.

This worry isn't vague. Retirement is the moment you start "drawing down" for the first time the money you've saved all your life. If the market happens to collapse at exactly that moment, the same drop hurts far more than it would at any other time. Today, let's look at why, and how you can prepare.

To give the conclusion first: this risk isn't fate you can't escape. It's a risk you can reduce in advance, by design.

## 1. The few years around retirement are the most dangerous stretch

Over thirty years of preparing for retirement, which year the market falls in doesn't make much difference. The problem is the period around retirement. It's the turning point from growing your savings to drawing on them, so the same drop has a far bigger impact on your total assets.

If you're in your 30s or 40s and still putting money in, you have time for the market to recover after a fall. But if you've just retired and are taking out living expenses every month, the only way to raise that money is to sell at the lowered prices. There's simply no room to wait for the recovery.

## 2. The order matters more than the return

Even with the same 30-year average return, falling sharply early and recovering later produces a completely different result from earning well early and falling later. Once you've started withdrawing, the order decides the outcome.

Here's a comparison. If you eat this year even the seed potatoes meant for next year's planting, then no matter how good the field becomes, there's nothing left to plant. If a down market forces you to sell many shares to cover living costs, you don't get the full benefit of the recovery when the market comes back. This is sequence risk — the reason a drop at the time you're withdrawing is especially damaging.

## 3. Set aside a part that won't shake even if the market falls

The way to reduce this risk is not to keep all your assets in one lump. Put the first few years of living costs after retirement somewhere you can draw from regardless of market swings, and split the rest into a structure that keeps growing. Then, even if the market shakes, you don't have to touch those few years of money.

One option here is to put part of your assets into products that pay a set amount by contract, such as fixed or indexed annuities. If you create a part that keeps paying a set income however the market moves, the rest of your assets gain room to wait for a recovery instead of being sold in a hurry during a downturn. I'm not saying to move everything this way. Even setting aside just the few years that fall in the danger zone makes a big difference to your peace of mind. Of course, these products also differ in contract terms and fee structures, so be sure to confirm with a qualified professional whether one fits your situation.

## [Plus tip] How many years is your "danger zone"?

Mark five years before and five years after your planned retirement date — ten years in all — as your danger zone. Then roughly write down how much you'll need to withdraw each year during those ten years. If you can set aside that total independent of market swings, the rest of your assets can keep growing for a long time with far more peace of mind. If the calculation feels overwhelming, don't struggle with it alone; taking the numbers you have now to a consultation is another option.

## Closing

You can't prevent the market from shaking someday. But how much it hurts if that shaking happens to coincide with your retirement depends greatly on the structure you set up ahead of time. Rather than worrying vaguely, take the next ten years and mark once which part needs protecting.
