> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, November 24, 2026.

For the past three months, "Ten Years to Retirement, One Step a Week" dealt with numbers and paperwork. You wrote things down one at a time, from living costs and Social Security to taxes and beneficiary lines. The new series starting today begins from a slightly different place: the stories changing around us, in work, family, education and the economy. When the world changes, the way we look at retirement has to change too.

The first installment is about work. These days, when I ask in consultations, "When are you planning to retire?", instead of a date I often hear, "Rather than quitting, I'd like to cut back a bit."

## 1. The shape of retirement has changed

For the previous generation, retirement was a single day. You had a farewell party on the day you reached retirement age, and the next day a life without work began. Today that line is blurring. Many people cut a five-day week to three days, continue their old company work as a contractor, or hand the store over to their children and go in only in the mornings.

The reason isn't only money. As lifespans have grown longer, the early 60s have become a time when people are still in their prime, and watching those who retired before them, people have learned that a day without any work is longer than they imagined. Work is income, but at the same time it's the rhythm of the day and a place to see people.

## 2. Working half-time changes the math

Continuing to work, even at reduced hours, has many benefits for a retirement plan. While you earn part of your living costs by working, you can delay when you start drawing from a 401k or IRA. As we saw in Part 4 of the previous series, you also gain room to delay claiming Social Security and increase what you'll receive for life.

There are two things to check in advance, though. One is that if you receive Social Security before full retirement age while working, part of your benefit is withheld when your earnings exceed a set amount. The other is health insurance. Reducing your hours can cost you eligibility for employer coverage, and until Medicare at 65, you'll need to arrange coverage separately. The increase in premiums can end up larger than the reduction in pay.

## 3. Plan for "the day you can no longer work," too

Half-time retirement is possible only while your health holds up. If a serious illness or accident makes you stop working earlier than expected, the loss of income and the rise in medical costs arrive at the same time. That's why a plan that says "I intend to work until 70" needs the question "What if I can't work before then?" attached to it.

This is where life insurance deserves a second look. It's easy to think of life insurance only as money your family receives after you're gone. But many of today's policies come with a "living benefits" rider that lets you receive part of the death benefit early, while you're alive, if you're diagnosed with a serious or chronic illness. Payout conditions vary by policy, so start by checking whether the policy you already own has this rider.

## [Plus tip] Write retirement as stages, not a date

Instead of a single line like "retire at age X," try writing it in stages. For example: as now until 62, three days a week from 62 to 66, and fully retired after that. Next to each stage, write the income that will come in and where your health insurance will come from, and the vague "someday" becomes a concrete plan.
