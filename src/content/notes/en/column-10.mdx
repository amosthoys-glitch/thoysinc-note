> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, October 20, 2026.

"If I don't need it, can't I just leave my 401k alone? I want to pass it on to my kids." I hear this from time to time in consultations. I understand wanting to leave it untouched and let it grow. But an account where tax has been deferred can't be left as it is forever. At a certain age, you must take out a minimum amount set by the government every year, and that money counts as income for the year. This is called a required minimum distribution, or RMD.

Last week, we saw that your money is divided into three pockets: regular, tax-deferred and Roth. This week is about the "clock" attached to the second pocket.

## 1. First, confirm when you have to start

The starting age has been pushed back repeatedly as the law has changed. Under current rules, if you were born between 1951 and 1959, it's 73; if you were born in 1960 or later, it's 75. Most readers about ten years from retirement fall under 75.

It applies to tax-deferred accounts such as a 401k, a 403b, a traditional IRA and a SEP IRA. A Roth IRA has no required withdrawals during the owner's lifetime, and starting in 2024, workplace Roth 401k accounts were removed from the rule as well. You can delay the first year's withdrawal until April 1 of the following year, but then you take out two years' worth in one year, and your income bunches together. That's exactly the tax-bracket problem I described last week. If you're still working at that age and own less than 5% of the company, you can delay the 401k at that employer until you retire. Old employers' 401k accounts and IRAs don't qualify for this, however.

## 2. The amount is set by your balance and your age

The calculation is simple. Divide the account balance as of December 31 of the previous year by a factor set by the IRS. The factor at 75 is 24.6. With a $500,000 IRA balance, the first year's required withdrawal is about $20,300. The factor shrinks as you age, so even with the same balance, the share you must take out grows every year. At 80 the factor drops to 20.2, which makes it about $24,750 on the same $500,000. Financial companies often calculate and tell you the amount, but the responsibility for taking it out on time is yours.

Add Social Security and other income on top of this, and it's easy to cross the Social Security tax thresholds and the Medicare premium surcharge thresholds we looked at last week. You end up forced to take out money you don't need and paying more in taxes and premiums.

## 3. Miss it, and the penalty is steep

If you don't take out the required amount by the deadline, a penalty of 25% of the shortfall applies. Miss $20,000, and the penalty alone is $5,000. Correct it within two years and it drops to 10%, but that's still money you could have avoided paying. If you have 401k accounts scattered across old employers, it's even easier to miss. With IRAs, you can take the combined total for several accounts out of one account, but with 401k accounts, you have to withdraw from each one separately.

## [Plus tip] The ten years before RMDs are your chance to reduce taxes

If you retire at 65, the roughly ten years until 75 are a period with no paycheck and no required withdrawals. In these low-income years, taking a little out of tax-deferred accounts within a low bracket, or moving it to a Roth, shrinks the balance — and the later required withdrawals shrink with it. Amounts moved to a Roth count as income that year, so decide with your tax professional how much is right each year. Also, after age 70½, donations sent directly from an IRA to a charity count toward your required withdrawal without being counted as income. The limit on these donations is adjusted every year.

## Closing

This week's homework is one thing. Using your and your spouse's birth years, work out the year your required withdrawals begin and write it on your calendar. Then divide the total of your tax-deferred account balances by 24.6 and write down what your first-year required withdrawal would be at today's balance. If that number is bigger than you expected, you now have something to prepare for over the next ten years.
