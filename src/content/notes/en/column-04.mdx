> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, September 8, 2026.

Writing down the five lines last week, you probably thought, "In the end, the only thing that reliably comes in every month is Social Security." That's right. So starting today, let's look at that first pillar.

At this point in a consultation, I hear two things most often: "You just apply as soon as you turn 62, don't you?" and "With government money, you come out ahead by taking it early." I understand both. The worry runs deep — what if you wait all that time and never get to collect?

But once you claim Social Security, that amount follows you for life. Far too many people decide in a few minutes something that deserves months of thought. Today, let's compare the numbers directly.

## 1. Same person, very different amounts

Social Security has a benchmark called full retirement age. If you were born in 1960 or later, it is 67. Claim at that age and you receive 100 percent of what your lifetime work record has earned.

Claim earlier and the amount is reduced; claim later and it grows. You can apply as early as 62, and at that point you receive 70 percent of the full amount. That's a 30 percent cut, and it doesn't come back when you later reach 67. On the other hand, if you wait until 70, the benefit grows 8 percent for each year, to 124 percent.

Let's look at the numbers. For someone whose benefit at full retirement age would be $3,000 a month:

Claim at 62 and you receive $2,100 a month. Claim at 67 and you receive $3,000 a month. Wait until 70 and you receive $3,720 a month.

Same person, same record — and the difference is more than $1,600 a month. For a married couple, that gap doubles.

## 2. So is waiting always better?

Not necessarily. While you wait, not a single dollar comes in. That's why you need to look at the break-even point.

Let's work it out with the example above. If you collect nothing for the eight years from 62 to 70, you give up about $200,000. From 70 on, you receive $1,620 more each month, and it takes about ten years for that difference to catch up with the $200,000. That's roughly age 80.

So the answer comes down to this. If you expect to live past 80, waiting comes out ahead; if you pass away before then, claiming early comes out ahead. That's why you need to look at your health and your family history together.

One thing does need to be said, though. The real risk in retirement planning isn't leaving this world early; it's outliving your money. If you live to ninety and the amount arriving each month is small, by then there's no way to undo it.

## 3. If you plan to keep working, the math changes again

If you claim before full retirement age while still working, and your earnings go over a set limit, $1 is withheld for every $2 above it. The limit is adjusted every year.

The withheld amount is gradually paid back after full retirement age, so it doesn't disappear entirely. But the money in your hands right now clearly shrinks. Every year, a few people who claimed at 62 and kept working come to me asking, "Why am I getting so little?"

If you plan to retire before 65, look at health insurance at the same time. Medicare starts at 65. Claiming Social Security at 62 doesn't start your health coverage along with it. How to fill that three-year gap is often the bigger problem in practice.

## [Plus tip] If you're married, don't calculate the two of you separately

When one spouse passes away, the survivor doesn't receive both benefits — only the larger of the two. So if the higher earner waits as long as possible, that larger amount becomes the surviving spouse's lifetime income. The lower earner can claim relatively early with less downside. A couple's claiming ages must be planned together.

## Closing

This week's homework takes ten minutes at a computer. Create an account at ssa.gov and bring back three numbers: what you'd receive at 62, at 67 and at 70. Write them at the bottom of the sheet you made last week.

Put those three numbers next to your gap and, for the first time, you'll see what those eight years of waiting mean for you. You'll be deciding with your own numbers, not someone else's advice.

Next week, we'll look closely at the spousal and survivor benefits I mentioned briefly today. A spouse who never held a job can receive them, and even after a divorce, if the conditions are met, you can claim on a former spouse's record. It's an area a great many people pass over without knowing.
