> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, December 1, 2026.

Last week, I talked about retirement as a "transition," not an "ending." Today is about the most down-to-earth worry for people in their 50s and 60s standing in that transition: family. When you are helping your parents and helping your children, your own retirement preparation tends to get pushed to the back. People caught between the generation above and the generation below, taking care of both, are often called the "sandwich generation."

## 1. More people are in the "sandwich generation" than you might think

In a survey released this August, Pew Research Center counted as "sandwiched" anyone who has a parent age 65 or older and either has a minor child or helped an adult child financially in the past year. One in four U.S. adults (25%) fits that description. Among people in their 40s it is 54%, and in their 50s, 45%. For the 50s, that is up sharply from 36% in 2021.

One more detail is worth noticing. Among sandwiched people in their 50s, 61% had no young children and were helping an adult child financially. The burden doesn't end when the children grow up. It changes shape.

## 2. Caregiving takes both time and money

According to the 2025 study by AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving, 63 million Americans, nearly one in four adults, provided ongoing care in the past year for an adult or a child with a complex medical condition or a disability. Close to one in four of those caregivers spends 40 or more hours a week on it, and nearly half have taken at least one major financial hit, such as going into debt or stopping their savings. AARP says caregivers spend roughly $7,200 a year of their own money.

When savings stop, the interest they would have earned stops too. If that happens in your 50s and 60s, the last ten years before retirement, it is hard to catch up later.

## 3. Decide your own share first

I am not telling you to stop helping. I am saying: set an order. First, decide in advance how much you will spend on your parents and your children, how much per month and on which things. With a limit in place, an emergency won't push you around. Second, take your retirement savings out first, like a fixed expense. If you try to save whatever is left over, there is usually nothing left.

Third, know what Medicare does not pay for in your parents' care. Medicare does not pay for long-term care. Everyday help such as bathing, dressing, and using the bathroom is not covered. So the cost of helping your parents can end up coming out of your own pocket.

"I don't want to be a burden on my children" is a promise that good intentions alone can't keep. You can keep it only if you have built your own income and preparation ahead of time.

## [Plus tip] Write down your parents' "five lines," too

In Part 3, you wrote down your own assets in five lines. This time, ask your parents to do the same. Which insurance policies and accounts do they have, where are the papers, and who is named as beneficiary? Put it all on one sheet, and when something suddenly happens, the first information you need will be in one place. If you tell them, "I want to know so I can help you," your parents will find it easier to say yes.

## Closing

This week's question is this: "Last year, how much did I spend on my parents and my children, and how much did I put into my own retirement savings?" Put the two numbers side by side with your spouse, and you will see how far your own retirement has been pushed back.
