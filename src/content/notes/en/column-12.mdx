> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, November 3, 2026.

"Even if my husband goes first, the benefits keep coming, so I'll manage." I hear this often in consultations. It's true that benefits keep coming. But the income the two of you received doesn't simply carry on, and taxes often get heavier instead. This combination — less income, more tax — is commonly called the "widow's penalty" in the U.S.

Last week, we talked about the cost of care. This week, let's look in numbers at how the household changes for the surviving spouse after one of a couple passes away first.

## 1. One of the two Social Security checks disappears

When both spouses receive Social Security and one passes away, the survivor receives only the larger of the two benefits. If the husband received $2,800 a month and the wife $1,500, after the husband passes, the wife receives only $2,800. Benefits that were $4,300 a month become $2,800 — $18,000 less a year.

That's why the longer the higher earner delays claiming, the better for the surviving spouse: the larger benefit earned by waiting becomes the survivor's benefit as it is. Conversely, if the higher earner started early, the amount the survivor receives carries on reduced by the same amount.

## 2. Withdraw the same amount, and you pay more tax

You can file jointly through the year your spouse passes away, but if you have no dependent children, from the following year you file as single. The bracket boundaries and standard deduction for single filers are roughly half those for married couples filing jointly. Income has dropped by about a third, but the brackets have narrowed by half, so the same withdrawal easily lands in a higher bracket. Someone who took out $30,000 a year as a couple and takes out the same $30,000 after being widowed may find a large part of it taxed one bracket higher.

The required withdrawals we saw in Part 10 also continue. If you roll your spouse's IRA into your own account, the balances combine, so the required withdrawal doesn't shrink. On top of that, the Social Security tax thresholds drop to $25,000 and $34,000 for single filers, and the Medicare premium surcharge thresholds are half those for couples. With reduced income, you end up paying more in taxes and premiums.

## 3. Living costs don't fall by half

With one fewer person, it seems living costs should halve, but in reality they don't. Property tax, home insurance, utilities and car upkeep stay almost the same. From my consulting experience, living costs after being widowed run about 70 to 80% of what they were as a couple. If a couple spent $6,000 a month, that means around $4,500 for one person. There are even new expenses, as you pay others to do things your spouse used to handle, like home repairs or driving.

In the example above, benefits fell by $18,000 a year, so the shortfall in living costs has to be filled entirely by taking more from retirement accounts. And that extra money is again counted as income at single-filer rates. Lower income, higher taxes and unchanged living costs all arriving at once — that's why the household gets tight after one spouse is left alone.

## [Plus tip] Look once more before you sign for a payout option

When you start receiving a company pension or an annuity, you choose between "single life" and "joint life." Single life pays a little more each month, but the payments end the day you pass away. For a company pension, this choice requires your spouse's written consent, yet quite a few people sign without fully understanding it. In most cases the choice can't be changed once made, so before signing, be sure to calculate what the surviving spouse's income would be.

## Closing

This week's homework is making two columns on a sheet of paper. Write "If the husband passes first" in one and "If the wife passes first" in the other, and under each, write the remaining benefits and income and the expected living costs. If you see a column that falls short, how to fill that difference is your task for the next ten years.
