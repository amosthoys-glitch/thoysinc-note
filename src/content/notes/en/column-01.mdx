> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, August 18, 2026.

In my consultations, there is one question I hear most from people in their late fifties: "How much do I need to retire?" And what comes next is almost always the same: "People say you need a million dollars."

When I hear that, I ask back: whose number is that million dollars? Someone whose house is paid off and whose children are on their own cannot have the same number as someone who still has a mortgage and tuition left to pay. When you aim for someone else's number, preparation never starts. Either it looks so big that you give up, or you reach it and still feel uneasy.

Retirement does not arrive automatically at sixty-five. It arrives the moment the money coming in each month can cover the money going out each month. That is why retirement comes by a number, not an age. Today, let's pin that number down ourselves. A sheet of paper and a pen are enough.

## 1. How much do you spend in a month right now?

The first thing to write down is your monthly living cost today — not what you expect to spend in retirement, but what actually goes out now. Include the mortgage or rent, the car, every insurance premium, groceries, utilities, and all the small things that come out on your cards each month.

Many people are surprised at this very first step, because the amount in their head and the amount actually leaving the account are different. It doesn't have to be exact. Skim the last three months of card statements and bank records and a rough picture will appear.

And this amount does not drop much just because you retire. More often, only the items change. I'll cover that separately next week.

## 2. How much of that will come in on its own after you retire?

On the next line, write down the money that will arrive every month after retirement without you doing anything. For most people, that is Social Security and nothing else. Create an account at ssa.gov and you can see your estimate right away. If you have never checked it, doing just this one thing this week is real progress.

If you have a company pension or already own an annuity plan, write that amount down too. Couples need to check each spouse separately. Spousal and survivor benefits are calculated differently again, so I will set aside a separate installment for them.

## 3. The difference is your number

Now subtract line 2 from line 1. What's left is the money you will have to draw from your own savings every month. I call it "the gap." It's a word that will come up throughout this series.

For example, if you spend $5,000 a month and Social Security pays $3,000, the gap is $2,000. Where will that $2,000 come from? Preparing for retirement is, in the end, the process of answering that one question.

This number isn't something you set once and forget. When the children move out or the mortgage is paid off, the gap shrinks. When medical costs rise, it grows. Recalculating once a year is enough.

## [Plus tip] Look at it as 25 years, not one month

A $2,000 gap looks manageable. But over a year it's $24,000, and if you retire at sixty-five and live to ninety, that's 25 years. Simple multiplication gives $600,000. Add rising prices, and you need more than that.

If that number feels big, that's normal. But you don't have to draw all of it from savings. If you set up income that pays every month for life, the gap itself shrinks or disappears. I'll walk through how, step by step, in the middle of this series.

One more thing. Drawing down what you've saved works against you the longer you live. Live to ninety-five, and it's 30 years. Changing that structure — where living a long life becomes the risk — is the real goal of retirement planning.

## Closing

Retirement often feels overwhelming not because you're unprepared, but because the goal isn't visible as a number. That overwhelmed feeling comes from not knowing. From the moment the number appears, it stops being overwhelming and becomes a plan.

This week, write down just three lines: living costs, Social Security, and the difference between them. Next week, we'll look at why those living costs don't fall after retirement — and which items actually go up.
