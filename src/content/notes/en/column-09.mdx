> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, October 13, 2026.

"Once I retire, I just take what I need out of my 401k, right?" I hear this often in consultations. It isn't wrong. But few people realize that the same $10,000 leaves you with a different amount in hand depending on which account you take it from, and in which year.

Last week, we talked about setting aside a part you won't touch even when the market shakes. This week is about what happens when you actually take that money out — in other words, taxes.

It's easy to assume that with no paycheck after retirement, there will be hardly any tax. But most of the money you take out of retirement accounts counts as income for that year. The order in which you withdraw is your tax plan.

## 1. Your money sits in three "tax pockets"

The first is regular bank and investment accounts. You've already paid tax on the money you put in, so there's no tax when you take out the principal; only interest and gains are taxed. The second is accounts where you deferred tax when you put money in, such as a 401k, a traditional IRA or a tax-deferred annuity. Money taken out of these — principal and earnings alike — is subject to income tax for that year, and taking it out before age 59½ adds a 10% penalty.

The third is accounts you funded after paying tax, such as a Roth IRA. Once you meet the requirements — being over 59½ and five years past your first contribution, among others — even the earnings come out tax-free.

If you do a direct rollover of an old employer's 401k into your own IRA or annuity, there's no tax on the move. But the money stays in the second pocket, so whatever you later take out is subject to income tax that year. Remember: only the pocket changes; the tax character doesn't.

## 2. Take it out all at once, and you move into a higher bracket

U.S. income tax rates rise as income rises. If you take a large sum out of the second pocket in one year, all of it is added to that year's income and taxed at a higher bracket. Take the same amount out over several years, and you can stay in a lower bracket each year.

As I've said in earlier columns, spreading it out doesn't make the tax disappear. It lets you pay less by spreading it out. The heart of withdrawal order is that by choosing how much to take from which of the three pockets, you can control the income recorded each year.

## 3. Withdrawals even touch Social Security taxes and Medicare premiums

This is a part many people miss. Social Security benefits can also be taxed if you have a lot of other income. For a married couple filing jointly, if adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest plus half of Social Security exceeds $32,000, up to 50% of the benefit becomes taxable; above $44,000, up to 85%. These thresholds haven't been adjusted for inflation in decades.

Medicare Part B premiums also rise with income. The 2026 premium is based on your income from two years earlier, in 2024, and a surcharge applies above $218,000 for couples or $109,000 for individuals. Take out a large sum in one year early in retirement, and your premium goes up two years later. Qualified Roth withdrawals, on the other hand, don't count as income and stay out of these calculations.

## [Plus tip] This week, add one more mark next to your asset list

Take out the asset list you wrote in Part 3 and write one of "regular," "tax-deferred" or "Roth" next to each line. Then find the adjusted gross income (AGI) on last year's tax return and write it alongside. The answer to which pocket to draw from first, and how much, is different for everyone, so take this list to your accountant or tax preparer and go over it together.

## Closing

How you take out your retirement money matters as much as how much you've saved. Simply separating the three pockets gives you a starting point for planning your taxes after retirement ahead of time.
