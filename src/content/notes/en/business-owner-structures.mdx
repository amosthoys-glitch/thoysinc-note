Once a business is established the question changes, from how to grow revenue to *how to keep what's left*. These four structures come up most often at that point.

## 1. Section 125 plan — do this first

Simply moving employee premium contributions to pre-tax cuts the company's payroll tax and the employee's income tax at the same time. It costs almost nothing to put in place and takes effect immediately, which is why it always comes first.

## 2. Executive bonus — holding onto key people

The company pays an employee's personal life premium as a bonus. The business deducts it like any other compensation, and because the policy belongs to the individual, it reads as a real benefit. Service conditions can be built into the design.

## 3. Key person insurance — protecting the company side

This covers what happens to the business if an owner or a critical producer is suddenly gone. The company owns the policy and receives the proceeds, which fund the cost of replacing them and cover the revenue gap. Lenders sometimes require it.

## 4. Buy-sell agreement — essential with partners

If a partner dies and their share passes to a spouse, the survivors end up running the company with a new partner who has never run one. A buy-sell agreement fixes in advance who buys that share and at what price, and life insurance is the usual way to have the money ready.

## Added later — three things that get missed when setting these up

*Added in November 2026.*

### A cafeteria plan starts with a document

The IRS defines a cafeteria plan as a **written plan** that lets employees choose between cash and pre-tax benefits. Telling staff "we'll take your premiums pre-tax" isn't enough; there has to be a plan document.

One more point: **a shareholder who owns more than 2% of an S corporation** is not treated as an employee for this purpose — they are treated like a partner. Whether you yourself can benefit depends on your entity type, so confirm it with your CPA.

### Key person coverage needs written employee consent — before the policy is issued

When the company owns the policy and is the beneficiary, it is "employer-owned life insurance" under the tax code. **Before the contract is issued**, the employee must be told in writing and must consent in writing:

- that the company intends to insure the employee's life, and the maximum face amount
- the employee's written consent to being insured, including that coverage may continue after they leave
- that the company will be a beneficiary of the death proceeds

Skip this, and the part of the death benefit **above the premiums the company paid can become taxable**. Companies holding these contracts also report the number of insured employees and the coverage amount on **Form 8925** with their tax return.

### Key person premiums aren't deductible

The tax code denies a deduction for life insurance premiums when the business is directly or indirectly a beneficiary. So key person premiums generally don't come off as an expense. In exchange — if the steps above were followed — the death benefit generally comes into the company tax-free. It is the mirror image of the 162 bonus described earlier, which keeps the two straight.
