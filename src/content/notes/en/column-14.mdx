> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, November 17, 2026.

Three months ago, in the first installment, I said that retirement arrives by a number, not an age. Since then, I've given you one piece of homework each week. If you've kept up, there are more than ten sheets of paper stacked on your desk by now. It's fine if you skipped a few weeks along the way.

Today, in the final installment of the series, let's bring those sheets together into a single retirement plan. Divide one sheet of paper into three sections. The top is the numbers, the middle is where the money is, and the bottom is dates and "what ifs."

## 1. Top section: how much are you short each month?

On the first line, write your monthly living costs after retirement. As I said in Part 2, include health insurance premiums and inflation, and estimate a little generously. On the second line, write your guaranteed income: Social Security at the claiming age you settled on in Parts 4 and 5, plus a company pension if you have one. The third line is the difference — the amount you'll need to fill from your own assets each month.

Add one more line here: how much of that difference is essential living costs that must go out no matter what, like food, housing and insurance premiums. As we saw in Part 6, it's safest to cover this part with income that pays for life regardless of the market. If it isn't covered yet, that amount is your first task for the next ten years.

## 2. Middle section: where is the money?

Copy over the asset list you made in Part 3. Next to each account, add three marks. The first is the tax pocket from Part 9: write whether it's tax-deferred, tax-already-paid like a Roth, or regular. The second is the beneficiary from Part 13: check that both primary and contingent are filled in. The third is the purpose I discussed in Part 8: mark whether it's money you'll draw on without touching it even if the market collapses in the years around retirement, or money you'll leave invested for the long term.

Once you've written it out, the same balances look different. If your money is concentrated in one pocket, or even the money you need soon is all in stocks, that's something to fix before retirement.

## 3. Bottom section: dates and "what ifs"

First, write the dates: the month your Medicare enrollment period begins (Part 7), the year you'll claim Social Security (Part 4), and the year your required withdrawals begin (Part 10). If you're married, write both spouses' dates. These are the days that, if missed, bring lifelong penalties or bigger taxes. Don't leave them only on the plan; copy them onto your calendar as well.

Next come the "what ifs." What if you need care — who will look after you, where, and which pocket will pay for it (Part 11)? What if one of you passes away first — what happens to the survivor's income and living costs (Part 12)? If the answer to either question is blank, circle that blank.

## [Plus tip] Rewrite it once a year, on the same day

The plan isn't a document you make once and are done with. Pick the same day each year, like a birthday or the end of the year, and update the numbers. Your Social Security estimate, account balances and living costs change every year. If something big happens, like a marriage, divorce, birth or death in the family, don't wait for that day — look at your beneficiaries again right away. Don't throw out last year's plan; keep them together, and in a few years you'll see at a glance how far your retirement preparation has come.

## Closing

Thank you for walking with me these fourteen weeks. Preparing for retirement isn't one big decision; it's filling in one sheet of paper, like today, again and again. Let me repeat what I said in the first installment: the overwhelmed feeling comes from not knowing, and from the moment the numbers appear, it becomes a plan.

This week's homework is to complete this one page and count the blanks you circled. The blanks aren't failures; they're your to-do list for the next ten years. If there's a space that's hard to fill alone, take this page to a professional and talk it through. With a plan in hand, the consultation becomes much shorter and more precise.

The series ends today. Thank you deeply for the attention you've given it.
