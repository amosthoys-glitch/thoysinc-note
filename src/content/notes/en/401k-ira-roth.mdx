401(k), IRA, Roth IRA. The names are the hardest part. All three are **accounts you put money in for retirement**. What makes them different from an ordinary savings account is a trade: the government gives you a tax break, and in return charges a penalty if you pull the money out early.

So why three names? Because of **when you pay the tax**. Get that straight and the rest is small print.

## The break-now accounts

A 401(k) and a traditional IRA. Whatever you put in this year, you pay less tax on this year. Later, when you take it out, you pay tax on it then. The tax isn't erased — it is **postponed**.

## The nothing-later account

That's the Roth IRA. You fund it with money you have already paid tax on, and when you take it out there is no tax at all — including on everything it grew into. If your income is high enough, this one may be closed to you.

Which is better comes down to a rough read. **Paying a lot of tax right now?** The break-now side usually wins. **Earning less now than you expect to later?** The nothing-later side usually does.

## If you work for a company — check this first

Many employers add a **match** to your 401(k): you put in $100, the company adds $50. Not claiming it is simply giving up pay. One question to HR — "what's our match?" — settles it.

One more thing. Money the company adds often requires a few years on the job before it is fully yours. If you are considering a move, check that alongside the offer.

## If you run a business

On your own, or with a spouse, a SEP IRA is the simplest thing that works. With employees, a SIMPLE IRA takes less handling, and a 401(k) comes in as you grow.

<Callout tag="Easy to miss">

People leave old accounts behind at former employers all the time. The money doesn't vanish, but nobody is watching it. When you move it, send it **account to account** — never to your own checking first. The moment it lands there, tax is withheld and a deadline starts running.

</Callout>

## How much can I put in?

There are limits, and they shift a little every year. That's why the numbers aren't printed here — the article would go stale. Take that year's figures from the IRS site or your HR department.

## The account and what goes in it are different questions

Everything above is about the **type of account**. What you put inside it to grow is a separate decision, and it is where paths diverge. I work with the option that does not lose principal — fixed and fixed indexed annuities — and do not handle stocks, funds, or other securities.

## Added later — three questions people ask first

*Added in November 2026.*

### When do the required withdrawals start?

That is the "required withdrawals" in the table. With a 401(k) or a traditional IRA, once you reach a certain age you must take out at least a set amount each year. Under the IRS rules today, that generally starts at **age 73**, and the first withdrawal can wait until **April 1 of the following year**. If you forget or take too little, the shortfall can be hit with a **25%** excise tax — reduced to **10%** if you fix it within two years.

There is good news too: **Roth IRAs and the Roth account inside a workplace 401(k) have no required withdrawals while you are alive.** If you don't need the money, you can leave it where it is.

### When can I take money out of a Roth tax-free?

Two things have to line up. It has been **five years** since the first year you put money into a Roth IRA, and you are **59½ or older**. (Disability, a beneficiary after death, or a first home — up to **$10,000** over your lifetime — can stand in for the age condition.)

Taking money out before then is less scary than it sounds. Withdrawals from a Roth are treated as coming **from your own contributions first**, and that part is not taxed. Tax only comes into it once you have taken out everything you put in and start touching the growth. (Money converted into a Roth from another account follows its own rules — if you have done a conversion, check with a tax professional.)

### Changing jobs: remember 60 days and 20%

This is why I said earlier not to take the money into your own hands.

- If your old 401(k) **pays the money to you**, **20% is withheld for taxes** — even if you plan to put it back
- You have **60 days** from the day you receive it to deposit it into a new account without tax. To get credit for the full amount, you must **make up the withheld 20% from other money**. If $2,000 of a $10,000 payout was withheld, you add $2,000 of your own to move the whole $10,000
- Moving money between IRAs this way is limited to **once a year**, counting all of your IRAs together

If the money goes **straight from account to account**, nothing is withheld, and neither the 60-day deadline nor the once-a-year limit applies. One sentence to the new HR department or the new account provider does it: "Please make it a direct rollover."

Rules can change, so check with a tax professional before you actually move or withdraw money.
