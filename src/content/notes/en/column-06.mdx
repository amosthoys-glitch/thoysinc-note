In my consultations, I often hear this: "Once I retire, I'll just take out what I need from my savings whenever I need it." That isn't wrong. But that approach carries a hidden risk: no one knows how long you'll live, or how the market will move in the meantime.

Take out again the Social Security amounts you confirmed over the past weeks and the gap from the asset list you wrote two weeks ago. Today, let's look at how that gap can be turned into a monthly paycheck that never runs dry.

## 1. The real risk is outliving your money

Keeping retirement assets in an account and withdrawing whenever you need to is called "systematic withdrawal." A commonly used guideline is 4 percent a year, but that number rests on the assumption that the market behaves like its average.

The trouble comes when the market falls sharply in the first few years after retirement. Say someone retires with a million dollars and keeps taking out $40,000 a year while the market drops 30 percent over the first two years. The remaining principal shrinks before it ever gets a chance to recover. Even at the same withdrawal rate, the balance twenty years later can be completely different depending on what the market did when you retired. This is called sequence-of-returns risk.

What makes it worse is that you can't know in advance when it will hit. Someone who retired in 2000 and someone who retired in 2010 may have started with the same amount and the same withdrawal rate, yet twenty years later their balances are worlds apart. There's no way to stop yourself from happening to retire in a bad year for the market. That's why a withdrawal strategy alone can't remove this risk completely; you have to decide separately, from the start, how much to expose to market swings.

## 2. Income comes from three sources with different natures

The first is money that pays a set amount for as long as you live, like Social Security. The second is money from a portion of your assets converted into lifetime payments, like a personal annuity. The third is investment assets you draw from as needed, such as a 401k or IRA.

The first two bring in a set amount no matter how the market moves. With the third, how much you can withdraw and for how long change with the market. If you lump all three together as "my retirement money" without telling them apart, it becomes hard to judge where to take how much from, safely, when you actually need it.

## 3. Lay the floor first, then build on top of it

Here is the order I recommend in practice. First, calculate your essential living costs — the things that must go out every month, like rent or the mortgage, food and insurance premiums. Then check whether that amount can be covered by guaranteed income alone, such as Social Security and pensions. This is the "floor."

Once the floor is in place, spending you can cut back on, like travel and hobbies, can come from your investment assets as circumstances allow. You can respond flexibly: a little more in good market years, a little less in bad ones. Without a floor, relying entirely on investment assets, you can't cut living costs even in a bad year, and you end up locking in losses at a time you didn't choose.

I call this split between the floor and flexible spending "two accounts." One brings in the same amount every month no matter how the market moves; the other grows and shrinks with circumstances. With the two separated, even when the news says the market has fallen, you don't have to worry about this month's rent and groceries. That makes up the largest part of peace of mind after retirement.

## [Plus tip] Factor in inflation, too

Social Security adjusts automatically for inflation each year, but fixed-payment annuities often keep the amount set at the start. That means the same amount twenty years from now won't have the same buying power it has today. When you design your floor, leave room for rising prices as well.
