> First published in Korean in my weekly newspaper column, November 10, 2026.

"My will is all done at the lawyer's office, so I have nothing to worry about." I hear this from time to time in consultations. Preparing a will was the right thing to do. But money that makes up a large share of retirement assets — a 401k, an IRA, life insurance, an annuity — doesn't follow the will. It follows the "beneficiary" line you filled in when you opened the account or bought the policy.

Last week, we looked in numbers at the surviving spouse's household after one of a couple passes away first. This week, let's talk about who receives your accounts and insurance after you're gone, and how.

## 1. The beneficiary line beats the will

Retirement accounts and life insurance are paid by the company directly to the beneficiary named on the form. Even if your will says "everything to my three children equally," if only your eldest son's name is on the 401k beneficiary line, that money goes to your eldest son. Bank and brokerage accounts can also carry a payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death (TOD) beneficiary.

Money that passes through a beneficiary designation doesn't go through the court's inheritance process, probate. That means you avoid a process that takes months, costs money and makes records public. In Arizona, you can get the same effect for your home by recording a beneficiary deed in advance.

## 2. Four mistakes I often see in consultations

First, the line is blank. With no beneficiary, the money goes into the estate and through probate. Second, there's a primary beneficiary but no contingent one. If the primary beneficiary has passed away first or dies in the same accident, it's the same as a blank line. Third, a former spouse's name is still there after a divorce. State law sometimes revokes it automatically, but accounts governed by federal law, like an employer 401k, may be paid to the name as written. Fourth, minor children are named directly. Minors can't receive the money themselves, so it's tied up until a court appoints a custodian, and they receive it all at once when they come of age. In that case, discuss naming a trust or a custodian as the beneficiary.

## 3. Consider the taxes for the person who inherits

Life insurance proceeds are usually received free of income tax. But when someone inherits a 401k or a traditional IRA, income tax applies each time they take money out. A spouse can roll an inherited IRA into their own account and continue it, but most children must empty the account within ten years. If required withdrawals, which we saw in Part 10, had already begun, there are also amounts that must be taken out every year during those ten years.

If a child in their peak earning years inherits a large IRA, a considerable part of it may go to taxes. Even for the same amount, receiving it through a Roth account or life insurance is far lighter for the child. Deciding which account to leave to whom is part of the plan.

## [Plus tip] For a 401k, the spouse comes first by default

For a married person, the law makes the spouse the default beneficiary of an employer 401k. To name anyone other than the spouse, the spouse must give written consent, notarized. If you've remarried and want to leave your 401k to children from a previous marriage, changing the form isn't enough; you must also complete this consent process. As with the payout options I mentioned last week, a single signature decides your family's share.

## Closing

This week's homework is taking out once more the asset list you wrote in Part 3. Add three more columns next to each account: primary beneficiary, contingent beneficiary, and the date you last checked. If any space is unknown, open the beneficiary screen on the company's or insurer's website to confirm it, then print that screen and keep it with your list. Whenever there's a marriage, divorce, birth or death in the family, look again.
